Top Headlines
Security

Kast Launches Sweeping Anti-Crime Agenda — and Needs the Opposition to Pass the Core of It

Fresh off his economic reform's Senate passage, President José Antonio Kast pivoted this week to his next signature fight: the ACOT (Agenda Contra el Crimen Organizado y el Terrorismo — Agenda Against Organized Crime and Terrorism), unveiled in a ~17-minute prime-time national broadcast on August 5. The centerpiece is a constitutional amendment to make public security an explicit state duty for the first time — backed by 28+ bills covering a new 5th state of exception to deploy the military for severe public-order crises, a national cartel designation list (where mere membership in a named criminal organization becomes a crime), extended police detention windows (12 to 24 hours for flagrante cases), tougher immigration controls, and border reinforcement with trenches, drones, and surveillance tech.

The problem: the constitutional amendment requires a 4/7 supermajority in Congress — roughly equivalent to a two-thirds Senate vote in the US — and the ruling coalition can't get there alone. Security Minister Martín Arrau says pre-legislative outreach to centrist opposition members has been underway; the target is Christmas passage. Congressional reaction has been predictably split: ruling-right lawmakers call it "a great news for Chile"; center-left opposition calls the measures "disjointed" and questions whether deploying the military for public order is the right model.

Economy

Megarreforma Tributaria Clears Congress — Now Faces Vetoes and a Constitutional Court Challenge

Chile's sweeping economic reform — cutting corporate taxes from 27% to 23%, adding VAT exemptions on home purchases, and offering tax incentives for capital repatriation — passed the Senate on August 4. But it's not law yet. The government immediately filed three presidential vetoes to strip out opposition-inserted provisions: a ban on compound interest (anatocismo), a "right to financial forgetting" that would erase old debt records after five years, and guaranteed 30-day payment terms for small businesses. Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz argued these "sound good but bring bad consequences." The Finance Commission of the Chamber of Deputies approved the three vetoes on August 5; full chamber votes follow next week.

A Constitutional Court review also looms after opposition senators filed formal requirements. Quiroz wants the reform enacted before the government submits its budget in late September. Think of it as a major US tax overhaul that passed both chambers but then got tangled in a presidential veto process and a Supreme Court challenge before it could actually take effect.

Politics

60 Deputies Pressure Kast to Pardon Officers Convicted for 2019 Protest Abuses

A bloc of 60 ruling-coalition legislators — from Kast's Republican Party, National Renewal (RN), UDI, Evópoli, and others — sent the president a formal letter this week demanding pardons for Carabineros (police) and military personnel convicted for actions taken during and after the October 2019 estallido social (social uprising). Republican lawmakers also launched a website called "Presos por Servir" (Prisoners for Service) listing nine individuals — including former officer Patricio Maturana and army sergeant Pedro Lavín — that collected more than 10,000 signatures within a single day.

The debate closely mirrors the US argument over pardoning officers convicted of excessive force during the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests — except Chile's 2019 uprising saw documented mass eye injuries from rubber pellets and other serious abuses that were investigated by the UN. Human rights organizations and the center-left warn pardons would signal impunity; the ruling right argues these individuals were performing their duty under crisis conditions and have been politically targeted. Kast's government is expected to review cases individually rather than issue a blanket pardon.

Chile's "Children's Day" Is Tomorrow — But the Law Says It Should Be in October

Tomorrow, Sunday August 9, is Día del Niño (Children's Day) in Chile — the day families give kids presents and toy stores run their biggest sales of the year. But here's the quirk: Chile's legal Children's Day, set by Decree 1,110, falls on the third Wednesday of October (October 21 this year). The August date is a retail-industry invention that moved the celebration to mid-winter to drive slow-season sales. It worked so well that August is now the de facto holiday and virtually no one marks October 21. Chilean kids effectively get two Children's Days: one the commerce world actually celebrates and one the government technically mandates. This weekend's FAMFEST at GAM — which runs through August 23 — and a wave of free family programming citywide are all timed to tomorrow's commercial holiday.

Opinion · Left

"Japan and Security as a Development Strategy"

El Mostrador · August 8, 2026 · Left-leaning

A columnist in the left-leaning El Mostrador uses Japan's 2026 national security white paper as a frame to critique Kast's ACOT: Japan integrates security policy with technological and industrial development — AI, robotics, cybersecurity — treating them as inseparable. The argument is that Chile's ACOT is heavy on punitive enforcement (longer detention, military deployment) and light on the structural investments — jobs, education, economic inclusion — that would actually reduce the conditions enabling organized crime to recruit. The column doesn't oppose fighting organized crime; it questions whether constitutional force alone can win.

"La Megarreforma Aprobada" — A Start, Not an End

El Líbero / UDD · August 2026 · Right-leaning

Market economist Klaus Schmidt-Hebbel — a leading voice of Chilean market orthodoxy, roughly the country's equivalent of a Heritage Foundation fellow — celebrates the megarreforma's congressional passage as "historic" in El Líbero and UDD's economics blog, but insists it's only a partial fix. The corporate tax cut and investment incentives are good; but Chile still needs to attack high marginal income-tax rates and public-spending inefficiency. His memorable line: "Hopefully there will be a megarreforma 2.0 next year." Lean: right; outlet: El Líbero (free).

FAMFEST 2026 — 33 Family Shows Across the City (Aug 8–23, Various Venues)

The 19th edition of FAMFEST (Festival de las Artes para la Familia) opens today with a free outdoor show in the GAM central plaza: "Fauna: una amistad incondicional" by OtroArte, featuring live music, aerial dance, acrobatics, and giant puppets — a good entry point for kids who've never been to a circus-style performance. Entry varies — today's plaza shows are free, ticketed shows start from around $3,000–8,000 CLP. Full schedule and tickets at famfestchile.cl and gam.cl.

Recent & Upcoming Releases
Rock

Los Tres — XCLNT

Still generating conversation four months after its April 10 release, XCLNT is the biggest Chilean rock event in a generation: the first album from Los Tres' original lineup in 26 years, recorded at Abbey Road Studios in London. The band — Chile's answer to the Pixies or Pavement, literary and angular with a blues undertow — sounds both continuous with their classic records and genuinely of 2026. Lead single "Cantar y Amar" is a slow-burn, emotionally devastating track that has introduced the band to a new generation of listeners. For fans of: 90s alternative, literary rock en español, Luis Alberto Spinetta. Essential.

Folk / Folklórico

Los Nogalinos — Patrimonial (Aug 19)

Coming August 19, Patrimonial is the new album from Los Nogalinos that explores Santiago's urban and railway history through the cueca — Chile's national musical form (think of it as deep-roots Chilean folk, somewhere between American old-time and New Orleans brass). The album weaves archival stories and street memory into a living musical form, and the pre-release buzz among folk fans has been strong. For fans of: Los Jaivas, cueca urbana, Nano Stern, traditional Chilean roots music. A welcome sign that the folk tradition is still actively generating new work, not just preserving old repertoire.
