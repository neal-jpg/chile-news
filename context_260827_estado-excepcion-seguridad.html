Chile's Proposed "Security State of Exception"

President José Antonio Kast's government has proposed amending Chile's constitution to create a new type of executive emergency power — called an estado de excepción constitucional de seguridad pública (public-security state of exception) — specifically designed to combat organized crime and drug cartels operating in Chilean territory.

What it is: Under the proposal, the president could unilaterally declare this security exception and deploy the armed forces, restrict freedom of movement, and limit the right of assembly — all without asking Congress first. The initial period would last up to 120 days, renewable for another 120 days (eight months total) before Congress would need to weigh in. Only extensions beyond that would require legislative approval.

How it works in Chile: Chile's constitution already allows for states of exception in cases of war, internal commotion, natural disaster, or public calamity — but each requires defined triggers and congressional involvement. This reform would carve out a new, president-controlled lane for security emergencies, with the military taking a lead role. Think of it as blending the Insurrection Act (U.S. law letting presidents deploy troops domestically) with a constitutional amendment locking that power in permanently.

The controversy: Critics from the left — and from within Kast's own center-right coalition — argue the proposal is too broad, lacks adequate oversight, and echoes powers wielded during Chile's 1973–1990 military dictatorship. The phrase "a lo Pinochet" surfaced in multiple editorials. As of late August, the government removed the bill's urgency and is negotiating changes: requiring congressional authorization from day one, with 30-day renewal reviews, and dropping the most sweeping provisions (communications interception, loss of social benefits).

Why it matters today: Chile has seen rising organized-crime activity, cartel violence in the far north, and street crime in Santiago. Kast won the 2025 election partly on a tough-on-crime platform. But whether a constitutional shortcut to military deployment is the right tool — and whether it can pass a four-sevenths Senate supermajority — is the defining political debate of his first year in office.
