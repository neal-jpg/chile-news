ACOT: Chile's New Constitutional Anti-Crime Package

What it is

ACOT stands for Agenda Contra el Crimen Organizado y el Terrorismo — Chile's most ambitious anti-organized-crime legislation since democracy was restored in 1990. President Kast submitted it to the Senate on August 10, 2026, requesting maximum urgency.

How it works in Chile

The package has two layers. The first is a constitutional amendment. Chile's constitution already lists specific "states of exception" — temporary crisis powers the president can invoke during war, natural disasters, or civil unrest. ACOT would add a sixth type: a security state of exception that could be declared in any territory controlled by a criminal organization. In those zones, the military could deploy alongside police, movement could be restricted, and authorities could fast-track asset seizures. The second layer is 28 separate bills covering new crimes, mandatory minimum sentences for cartel leaders, a national blacklist of criminal organizations, and separate maximum-security prison wings for gang bosses.

The American analogy

Imagine combining the RICO Act (treating organized crime as an enterprise you can prosecute as a whole), the National Emergencies Act (presidential powers to declare crises), and a zone-targeted version of the Insurrection Act — all bundled into one push and written directly into the constitution. The closest US parallel is how some border-state governors have used state emergency powers to deploy the National Guard to gang-controlled zones, except Chile is trying to enshrine this at the constitutional level rather than rely on executive discretion.

Why it matters right now

Chile has seen a sharp rise in organized-crime violence since roughly 2020, driven partly by Venezuelan cartels (most notably Tren de Aragua) expanding south and by Chilean gangs that grew up in their shadow. Contract killings, business extortion, and drug trafficking have rattled public confidence. Kast ran heavily on security and promised to give the government new tools to reclaim territories where gangs have effectively replaced the state. Critics warn the new "state of exception" could become a blank check for military deployment in poor neighborhoods, echoing concerns raised during Chile's use of similar powers during the 2019 protests.
