Top Headlines
Politics

Kast's Security Reform Stalls as Coalition and Opposition Both Revolt

President José Antonio Kast filed eight constitutional amendments to the Senate on August 17 under Chile's fastest legislative track — a 15-day clock that forced an immediate debate. The centerpiece is a new presidential "state of public security exception": Kast could declare it unilaterally for up to 120 days, renew it once without Congress (240 days total), with lawmakers only getting a veto if he tries to extend it further. Critics on both sides call it an unprecedented executive power grab.

What makes this rare is the cross-aisle unity against the bill. Former president Gabriel Boric (center-left) said on August 19 it's "a limitation on liberties not seen before in Chilean democracy." Evelyn Matthei — the right's own 2025 presidential candidate — said anyone expecting a constitutional fix is "completely wrong." Even Kast's own coalition is fracturing: UDI's Guillermo Ramírez (his party's closest ally, think the Republican establishment) says the bill "damages the institutional order," and four RN deputies filed eight amendments of their own before agreeing to vote yes. BioBioChile's headline — "A lo Pinochet" — captured the street-level sentiment. The government needs 29 of Chile's 50 senators; they are not there.

The government's defense: homicide rates are down 17.4% so far in 2026 under the existing ACOT security agenda. The constitutional reform, they argue, is simply the legal foundation those results deserve. But at this point, the bill appears more likely to be renegotiated than passed as filed.

Economy

Kast's Economic Mega-Reform Signed Into Law After Narrow Congressional Vote

Chile's Congress approved Kast's sweeping economic reform package in early August after months of debate — a centerpiece of his "Chile open for business" agenda. The law amends 36 statutes and 15 decrees: the headline provision cuts the corporate tax rate from 27% to 23% by 2029 (bringing Chile closer to regional competitors), fast-tracks permits for major infrastructure, and simplifies investment rules across multiple sectors.

Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz called it a signal that Chile is committed to long-term investor certainty. But the vote was close, and three articles were stripped before passage — including a controversial "financial right to be forgotten" provision. Analysts say the narrow margin raises credibility questions: will future governments honor these rules? That uncertainty makes the reform a credibility test as much as a policy change. Critics also note that Chile's economy contracted 0.5% in Q1 2026 with unemployment at 9.4%, and question whether a corporate tax cut alone will drive the investment needed to reverse that trend.

Security

Kast's Own UDI Party Splits Over Emergency Powers — Reform's Fate Unclear

The details of the coalition fracture matter: the UDI (Kast's core base, think the traditional Republican establishment) and RN (the moderate conservative wing) are both divided over the 240-day state of exception provision. The Partido Nacional Libertario (libertarian allies) said the text needs hard limits or should be rejected outright. RN's party president withheld support, saying the government "did too little preparatory work" before filing the bill. Four RN deputies are pushing their own amendments.

The government's 15-day urgency clock means a Senate vote must happen soon. Behind the scenes, officials are quietly negotiating — most likely to strip or modify the second-extension provision that triggered the sharpest backlash. If a slimmed-down version passes, the government can claim a win; if negotiations fail, the reform dies in its current form and returns as a political liability.

Chilean Architect Smiljan Radić Won Architecture's "Nobel Prize" — Did You Catch It?

Back in March, Santiago-based architect Smiljan Radić Clarke was named the 2026 Pritzker Prize laureate — the "Nobel of architecture." Radić, 61, is known for his experimental approach: a restaurant balanced on giant boulders in Santiago, a donut-shaped pavilion in London. The jury, chaired by Chilean architect Alejandro Aravena (himself a past winner), praised Radić's work for "embracing uncertainty and cultural memory over unwarranted certainty." He's only the fifth Latin American architect to win in the prize's 47-year history, and the connection to Santiago is direct — several of his signature buildings are here.

"The Problem with the School Lottery Isn't the Lottery"

Fernando Schmidt argues in El Líbero that Chile's school admissions lottery (the SAE — Sistema de Admisión Escolar) is misunderstood by its critics. The lottery itself isn't the flaw; the flaw is that one random draw effectively determines your child's entire educational trajectory, with almost no corrective mechanism afterward. Schmidt advocates for more flexibility in the system — secondary re-matching, school-of-choice provisions, and reducing the finality of the initial assignment. Think of it as the difference between a US school-district assignment (lottery just picks the public school) versus a market where you can keep shopping. His take aligns with Kast-era education policy but comes from a more technocratic, less ideological angle than typical right-leaning commentary.

BioBioChile / Left perspective · Aug 20–23

"When Boric and Matthei Agree, You've Lost the Room"

The left's critique of Kast's security reform isn't just ideological — it's constitutional. Columnists and analysts at BioBioChile, The Clinic, and El Mostrador point to the same core problem: a 240-day executive state of exception with no meaningful Congressional check until the second renewal is structurally incompatible with Chilean democracy post-1990. The Pinochet comparison isn't just political theater — Chile's constitution was written with military-era emergency power abuses explicitly in mind. The fact that Matthei (Kast's rival from the right) echoes Boric (his rival from the left) signals that this particular provision crossed a line that transcends ideology. Left-leaning commentary this week has focused less on opposing the security agenda itself — crime is genuinely down — and more on insisting that results don't justify constitutional shortcuts.

Rock Indie

Hesse Kassel — Morir Saltando

The most-discussed Chilean rock album of the season dropped July 15 and is still generating heat ahead of the band's headlining slot at Festival Neutral (Sept 26). Morir Saltando is their second studio album — a deliberately rawer, more visceral record than their debut La Brea (2025). The band recorded largely live in-studio with all six members playing simultaneously, chasing the energy of a live set rather than a polished production. The result is the kind of album that sounds like it was recorded in one adrenaline-soaked week. Hesse Kassel won Best New Artist at Chile's Pulsar Awards this year; Morir Saltando is them stepping out of that lane. For fans of early Pile, Fontaines D.C., or Chile's own La Ley on a rougher day.

Los Prisioneros — "We Are Sudamerican Rockers" Limited 7"

Also on Día del Vinilo (Aug 15): a numbered limited-edition 7" pressing of "We Are Sudamerican Rockers," the classic track from Chile's most influential rock band. Los Prisioneros formed in the early 80s and became the soundtrack of a generation protesting Pinochet-era oppression — the band's politics were inseparable from the music. This 7" is a collector's release; it's not a new recording, but the vinyl format (with that specific song) signals something — a marker for a band whose name keeps resurfacing at politically tense moments in Chile.
