What Is the ACOT? Chile's Sweeping Anti-Crime Agenda, Explained

The ACOT — Agenda Contra el Crimen Organizado y el Terrorismo (Agenda Against Organized Crime and Terrorism) — is President José Antonio Kast's flagship second-year security package, announced in a prime-time national broadcast on August 5, 2026. It's a combined constitutional amendment plus a stack of 28+ bills all aimed at combating organized crime.

What's in it? The centerpiece is a constitutional amendment that would, for the first time, explicitly make it the Chilean state's duty to protect public security — a subtle but legally significant change that would broaden the tools available to police and prosecutors. Alongside that, the ACOT includes:

A new 5th state of exception (think executive emergency powers) that lets the president deploy the military for serious public-order crises — currently the military can only be deployed for natural disasters or external threats. An official cartel designation list, where membership in a named criminal organization becomes a crime in itself. Extending the window for holding someone caught in the act of a crime (flagrante delicto) from 12 to 24 hours. Tougher immigration detention for undocumented arrivals. Border reinforcement with trenches, drones, and surveillance technology. A national asset-seizure agency to strip criminal organizations of property.

American analogy. Imagine a federal "tough on crime" package in the US that combines elements of the USA PATRIOT Act's expanded powers with Arizona-style immigration enforcement — but requires a two-thirds Senate majority to pass the core piece. That's roughly ACOT's situation: the constitutional amendment needs a 4/7 supermajority in each chamber (roughly 84 of 138 deputies, 29 of 50 senators). Kast's coalition doesn't have that alone, so he needs centrist opposition votes.

Why now? Chile has seen a documented rise in organized crime — gang activity linked to Tren de Aragua (the Venezuelan transnational gang) and others — over the past four years. Kast ran heavily on security; after passing his economic reform, this is his next signature battle. The government wants the full package enacted before Christmas 2026.

Critics — including the center-left opposition and human rights organizations — worry about mission creep, militarization of public order, and whether a 5th state of exception could be misused. Supporters argue ordinary policing tools are no longer adequate for the scale of organized crime Chile now faces.
