Who owns the Strait of Magellan — and why is Chile upset?

The Strait of Magellan is one of the world's most strategically important waterways: a roughly 350-mile passage at the southern tip of South America connecting the Pacific and Atlantic oceans. Think of it as the Panama Canal of the southern hemisphere — ships have used it for centuries to avoid the treacherous Drake Passage rounding Cape Horn.

Chile has held uncontested sovereignty over the strait since the Boundary Treaty of 1881, when Chile and Argentina formally divided Patagonia. Under that deal, Chile kept the strait entirely. The Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1984) — signed after the two countries came close to war over islands near Cape Horn — reaffirmed the same borders. Both treaties are considered settled international law.

So when Argentina's Air Force Chief of Staff, General Gustavo Javier Valverde, said in a podcast interview in late August 2026 that "Magallanes, the south, the Drake — all of that is sovereignty" for Argentina, it landed like a diplomatic grenade in Santiago. Chile's foreign minister, Francisco Pérez Mackenna, immediately filed a formal protest note and publicly stated the issue was "beyond debate." The mayor of Punta Arenas — the Chilean city that sits at the strait's entrance — called Argentina's response "not spontaneous" and suggested a deliberate provocation.

American analogy: Imagine a senior US military official going on a podcast and casually claiming the Panama Canal belongs to the United States under an old historical claim — while the US and Panama have a signed treaty saying otherwise. That's roughly the diplomatic equivalent of what happened here.

Argentina's foreign ministry quickly walked the general's comments back, saying their official position is exactly what the 1881 and 1984 treaties say. But the incident has stoked genuine concern in Chile about whether Argentina's military and government are on the same page — especially as both countries are led by right-wing administrations with a complicated bilateral relationship.
