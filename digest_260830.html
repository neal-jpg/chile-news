Top Headlines
Security

Kast's Anti-Crime Reform Hits Senate Wall — Government Pulls Fast-Track

President Kast's centerpiece security package — the ACOT constitutional reform — stalled badly this week. The government yanked its "suma urgencia" (congressional fast-track) designation on August 25 after the bill arrived in the Senate without enough votes even from ruling coalition legislators who complained they hadn't been consulted. The proposal would create a new "State of Exception for Organized Crime" giving the president power to restrict movement, assembly, and communications for up to 240 days without a congressional vote — roughly equivalent to the US president declaring martial lite in a neighborhood without congressional approval. Opposition parties are uniformly against it, and a new Cadem poll published Sunday shows 49% of Chileans think the bill should be pulled back for revision before any vote.

The government has pivoted to prioritizing 30 separate security-related bills already in the pipeline rather than burning political capital on the constitutional amendment. Polling also showed this week that 47% of Chileans now say the country is "going backwards" — up from just 31% in March, when Kast took office.

Economy

Unemployment Hits 9.5% — Worst in Five Years, Nearly One Million Out of Work

Chile's National Statistics Institute (INE) published new labor data Friday showing unemployment reached 9.5% in the May–July quarter — the highest rate in five years, excluding the pandemic. The employed population actually shrank 0.2% in the past twelve months, the first outright job contraction since early 2021. Around 184,000 Chileans have been searching for work for over a year, double the proportion of long-term unemployed compared to a decade ago, and the average job-search duration hit 8.1 months.

This isn't the full "technical recession" Chile dipped into this spring (two quarters of negative GDP growth), but it's a real-world jobs version of that same slowdown. The labor market had been slow to respond; now it is. Kast's team frames the cause as weak inherited investment from the Boric era; critics say the government's own $6 billion in spending cuts are making it worse.

Security

Off-Duty Carabinero Killed in Renca, Reigniting Police Self-Defense Debate

Officer Alejandro Ortiz, 32, married with two young children, was shot in the face and killed Thursday evening while off duty in the El Perejil neighborhood of Renca — a working-class commune north of Santiago. He had apparently gone there to complete a sale arranged through social media and was ambushed: when the buyers refused to pay and one pulled a knife, Ortiz reached for his service weapon (which off-duty officers are permitted to carry), and a second assailant shot him at point-blank range. Three suspects have been detained; three more remain at large.

The case has revived a long-running debate: should off-duty police have clearer legal protection — something like a US "castle doctrine" — when they use force in self-defense? Critics argue the current framework leaves officers vulnerable, while others say arming police during civilian errands creates its own risks. The Kast government has made police protection a signature issue.

Politics

Kast Approval Stuck at 35% as "Going Backwards" Sentiment Nearly Doubles

A Criteria Research poll published mid-August puts President Kast's approval at 35% with 55% disapproval — broadly unchanged from the prior month but a stark shift from his March inauguration honeymoon. More telling: the share of Chileans who believe the country is "going backwards" has grown from 31% to 47% in five months. Think of it as Chile's "right track / wrong track" number — and it's moving in the wrong direction fast. Kast's base sees his spending cuts and security push as necessary corrections; center and left-leaning Chileans increasingly see inflation, unemployment, and crime all getting worse at once.

Opinion & Editorial
El Desconcierto · Left

"La ideología de la excepción" — The ACOT's Real Agenda

A columnist at El Desconcierto argues that the ACOT security reform isn't really about fighting cartels — it's about elevating "public order" to a constitutional level that ranks above social rights like assembly and free movement. The column notes that the bill's most substantive article concerns who can access free education after serving a sentence, not actual tools against money laundering or border crime. The author draws a comparison to post-9/11 US legislation: measures sold as anti-terror tools that end up being used against protesters, strikers, and dissenters. The left-wing critique: Kast is building a legal architecture of exception that future administrations could use in ways nobody is discussing right now.

El Líbero · Right

Chile's Constitution Has No Tools Against Organized Crime — That's the Problem

El Líbero's defense of the ACOT reform is straightforward: Chile's current constitution was written for a different era, and the arrival of Venezuelan Tren de Aragua, Chilean narco networks, and extortion mafias in working-class neighborhoods has exposed a genuine legal gap. The Chilean state currently cannot restrict movement, impose curfews, or intercept communications in a crime-controlled area without declaring a full-blown state of constitutional emergency — a tool calibrated for coups and natural disasters, not gang territories. The column argues critics are conflating the architectural risk of expanded powers with the immediate, concrete failure of the state to protect citizens in places like Villa Francia or Quilicura today.

The Chilean trio Sistemas Inestables introduces a new chapter with Vestigium, built from synthesizer layers, raw winds, and a driving, textured drumkit — electronic progressive in the tradition of Tangerine Dream or Klaus Schulze but with a distinctly Andean spaciousness to the sound design. Chart Snapshot — Urbano Continues to Dominate Streaming

Chilean Spotify charts remain heavily reggaeton and urbano — Bad Bunny, Bizarrap sessions, and local urban artists dominate the top slots. That's the mainstream current; the releases above all sit outside it. Worth knowing the landscape even if it's not your vibe: the urbano dominance is why Chilean indie, rock, and electronic artists increasingly distribute internationally first, then build domestic audiences — a reversal from the 1990s era when Los Tres and La Ley built local fanbases that projected outward.
