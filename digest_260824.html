Politics

Kast's Security Reform Stalls in the Senate — Even Coalition Allies Are Skeptical

President José Antonio Kast's marquee anti-crime legislation is in serious trouble. The proposed constitutional amendment — which would create a new "Security State of Exception" allowing military deployment in crime-ridden areas for up to 240 days — arrived in the Senate last week with maximum urgency, but the government doesn't have the 4/7 supermajority (29 votes) it needs. Coalition partners Evópoli and UDI are both raising objections, and the opposition has rejected it outright.

The criticism spans the political spectrum. Evópoli president Luciano Cruz-Coke said the reform grants executive powers "I would never want in opposition hands" — the kind of constitutional caution you'd hear from a US senator worried about future administrations abusing crisis authorities. Former President Boric called it "an unprecedented limitation of freedoms in Chilean democracy." The director of Chile's Human Rights Institute (INDH) went further, warning it represents a rights rollback not seen "since the end of the dictatorship." The government signals openness to modifications, but so far those concessions haven't been enough to flip the needed votes. The reform needs to clear Senate committees (Constitution, Security, and Defense) before a floor vote — and the calendar is tight.

Economy

Congress Passes Kast's Growth Package — Tax Cuts and Investment Incentives Now Law

Chile's Congress approved a broad economic stimulus bill this week, featuring corporate tax cuts and new investment incentives aimed at rebooting growth in the world's largest copper producer. The legislation cleared Congress after months of negotiations and arrives as the economy's June IMACEC (Chile's monthly economic activity gauge, like a mini-GDP reading) showed 2.4% growth, narrowly avoiding what would have been a technical recession.

Analysts still expect only 1.3–1.4% annual growth for 2026, meaning the new measures won't deliver visible results until next year at the earliest. Finance Minister Quiroz called the June IMACEC "a thing of the past," insisting better numbers are ahead — though that optimism is doing work while households deal with rising fuel costs (see below).

Economy

Fuel Prices Jump After Three-Week Freeze — Up to 90 Pesos on Diesel

Gasoline rose about 30 pesos per liter and diesel up to 90 pesos on August 20, when the government's price freeze ended. Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz has defended the increases as unavoidable, citing a global conflict's effect on import costs and framing them as consistent with fiscal responsibility. The government introduced targeted relief measures for vulnerable households, but for most Chileans the hike lands directly on the household budget just as winter heating costs peak.

For scale: 90 pesos on diesel is roughly a US dime per liter — smaller than American pump swings, but significant in Chile where diesel moves freight and keeps the public bus system running. Retailers and distributors are likely to pass those costs along in the coming weeks.

Rare Heavy Snow in La Araucanía — First Big Accumulation in a Decade

Loncoche, a town in La Araucanía about 700 km south of Santiago, saw its heaviest snowfall in roughly ten years this week, surprising residents who live well outside Chile's usual Andean snow belt. The south was already having a rough weather week: tornados struck four communes in the Ñuble region, damaging homes and injuring one person. Chile's winter is serious business once you're past the capital.

Opinion · Left

"An Unprecedented Risk of Setback Since the End of the Dictatorship"

The Clinic · INDH Director · Left

Chile's government-funded Human Rights Institute (INDH) issued its sharpest warning yet against Kast's constitutional security reform: granting the president 240-day emergency powers with minimal congressional oversight represents, in the director's view, the most serious civil liberties rollback since Pinochet left office in 1990. The argument isn't that organized crime isn't real — it's that the tool is disproportionate and creates a template any future executive could exploit. This is a left-leaning reading, but one resonating across ideological lines this week.

Opinion · Right

"Powers I Would Never Want in Opposition Hands"

El Líbero · Evópoli · Center-Right

Writing from the right, government coalition members from the liberal Evópoli party have broken ranks publicly. Evópoli president Luciano Cruz-Coke's critique — that the new executive crisis powers are ones "I would never want in opposition hands" — is essentially the Madisonian argument applied to Chilean politics: design your institutions for bad actors, not just good ones. The UDI's position is similar but distinct: they object that the reform "damages institutionality" as written. It's notable that Kast's most direct obstacle right now isn't the opposition — it's his own coalition.

Releases & Scene
Rock Folk/Folklórico

Los Tres — XCLNT

Still the biggest story in Chilean music in 2026: Los Tres released their first album with the original lineup in 26 years — recorded at Abbey Road in London with Álvaro Henríquez, Ángel Parra Jr., Roberto Lindl, and Francisco Molina. Released April 10, XCLNT blends the band's signature folklórico textures with mature songwriting that rewards returning to. Singles "Cantar y Amar" and "Como llegaste te vas" are streaming everywhere. For fans of: Tom Waits, Los Lobos, vintage Santana. If you haven't listened yet, fix that today.
