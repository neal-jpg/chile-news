Chile Is Retiring Its Multi-Fund Pension System. What Does That Mean?

The short version

Chile's retirement savings system runs through private fund managers called AFPs. For years, AFP members could choose to invest their savings across five different funds — labeled A through E — ranging from aggressive to conservative. That system, the multifondos, is being phased out. Starting April 2027, it will be replaced by Fondos Generacionales (Generational Funds), which automatically place your savings in the right risk level based on your age.

How multifondos worked

Think of Chile's AFP system like a 401(k) that the government set up for everyone. Instead of just picking "stocks" or "bonds," workers chose from Fund A (most aggressive — mostly stocks, highest potential return) down to Fund E (most conservative — mostly bonds, lowest risk). Younger workers were encouraged to pick A or B. Retirees typically moved to D or E. The problem: millions of people never chose, or kept bad allocations, or panicked and switched funds at the worst times during market crashes.

The closest U.S. analogy is target-date retirement funds — mutual funds that automatically rebalance from aggressive to conservative as you near retirement. The Vanguard Target Retirement 2040 Fund works this way. The new Chilean system does the same thing, but it's now mandatory for everyone in the AFP system.

What changes in April 2027

The five A–E funds are replaced by a single "generational fund" matched to your birth year. Workers born around 1980 get a moderately aggressive mix; those born around 1960 get a more conservative one. The fund automatically shifts as you age — you don't manage it. The Investment Regime (the rule book for how the funds are invested) is currently in public consultation, closing July 31, with the final version expected in September 2026.

Why it matters

This affects every Chilean who has an AFP account — essentially most formal-sector workers. The change is meant to take the guesswork out of a system that, in practice, left too many people in the wrong fund for too long. Critics worry about reduced flexibility; supporters say the behavioral evidence is clear that autopilot beats active switching for most people. No action is required for most workers — the system migrates automatically.
