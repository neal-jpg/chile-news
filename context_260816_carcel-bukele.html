Chile's "Bukele-Style" Maximum-Security Prison

What is it? On August 13–14, 2026, Chile's government transferred roughly 700 high-risk inmates to La Laguna, a retrofitted maximum-security facility in Talca, 160 miles south of Santiago. The operation — dubbed "Operación Cancerbero" — was broadcast live on official channels, with aerial drone footage, convoy graphics, and real-time commentary. It is explicitly modeled on El Salvador's approach to incarceration under President Nayib Bukele.

Who is Bukele, and what did he do? Nayib Bukele is El Salvador's president, re-elected in 2024 after a first term defined by a mass crackdown on the MS-13 and Barrio 18 gangs. In 2022 he declared a state of emergency and had over 75,000 people arrested in two years; he also built CECOT (Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo), a mega-prison for 40,000 inmates, filmed its operations for maximum media impact, and saw El Salvador's murder rate plummet. Critics (the UN, human rights groups, US State Department) documented mass due-process violations; supporters point to the dramatic drop in violence as proof the approach works. Bukele is enormously popular in Latin America among voters exhausted by gang crime.

How does it work in Chile? La Laguna's regime mirrors CECOT's principles: mandatory matching uniforms, shackled transport, strict segregation from the general prison population, heavy surveillance, and restricted family visits. The goal is to deny gang leaders the ability to run criminal operations from behind bars — a real problem in Chile, where Tren de Aragua and other organized crime groups have continued coordinating crimes via cell phones in ordinary prisons.

American analogy. Think of it as a cross between a federal Supermax (ADX Florence, Colorado — the US's most restrictive prison) and the performative optics of a televised police raid. Chile doesn't have a federal Supermax equivalent; most inmates have served in underfunded, overcrowded state prisons where gang hierarchies go largely unchallenged.

Why it matters today. President Kast used the operation as the most visible symbol of his security agenda, which includes 30 new bills and a proposed constitutional amendment. The opposition calls it theater; supporters say it's necessary. The bigger constitutional fight — whether Chile's independent human rights watchdog (INDH) can monitor the new facility — has turned into its own political crisis.
