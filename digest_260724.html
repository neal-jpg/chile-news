Trade · International

Trump Slaps 12.5% Tariff on Chilean Exports — Effective Today

The Trump administration raised the tariff rate on Chilean goods from 10% to 12.5%, effective July 24, citing Chile's "insufficient efforts to combat forced labor." The order covers wine, salmon, fresh fruit, and agro-industrial exports — essentially anything that competes on price in a US supermarket aisle. Copper, Chile's biggest export to the US, is carved out under a separate national-security review.

Santiago flatly rejected the rationale. The government noted the decree does not allege that Chilean goods are actually produced under forced labor; it objects to Chile's legal framework for prohibiting such imports. The SNA — Chile's national farm lobbying organization, think the American Farm Bureau — called the tariff a blow to "competitiveness and certainty for thousands of producers." Officials said they would immediately seek exemptions for key products. Chile and the US have had a Free Trade Agreement since 2004 that had eliminated these duties; Trump's executive orders are effectively overriding it.

Politics · Coalition

Ex-Spokeswoman's Bombshell Sends Kast Scrambling for Coalition Unity

Mara Sedini — Kast's former government spokesperson who lasted just 69 days before being pushed out — went public this week with a scorching interview, accusing the administration of operating through "lies, schemes, and harassment." She said she lost her authenticity under pressure from the presidential palace's "Second Floor" (La Moneda's West Wing equivalent, the inner political staff). Chile Vamos coalition partners called her disloyal and "without codes" — the Chilean way of saying she broke the first rule of cabinet loyalty. Kast held a second emergency coalition coordination meeting today in response.

The opposition was gleeful. PDG legislator Pamela Jiles called the interview "probably the most important in five years." It also lands in a complicated moment: a new Criteria poll shows Kast's approval ticked up 5 points to 37% — his first rise in five weeks — suggesting the Sedini drama hasn't yet moved public opinion, though the latest survey predates the interview's publication.

Economy · Budget

Quiroz Defends Deep Budget Cuts as Chile's GDP Forecast Falls to 1.8%

Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz is defending a framework for the 2027 national budget that proposes cutting or discontinuing hundreds of social programs, including up to $427 billion pesos (about $450M USD) from elderly-benefit programs serving more than 900,000 Chileans. Critics — including investigators at CIPER Chile, a watchdog outlet — noted that 40 of the 401 plans on the chopping block directly contradict Kast's campaign promises. The ministry's line: "discontinuing" a program means redesigning it, not axing it.

The backdrop is a genuine fiscal squeeze. The government has revised Chile's 2026 GDP growth forecast down to 1.8%, and the structural deficit stands at 2.6% of GDP. Think of it like the US running a structural deficit and a new administration proposing to cut Medicare in the out-year budget to close the gap — politically radioactive, but arithmetically hard to avoid if you're also committed to not raising taxes.

Public Safety · Data

Annual Crime Survey Shows Chileans Still Feel Unsafe — Even as Victimization Dips

The INE (Chile's census bureau) and the crime-prevention agency published the ENUSC 2025 — Chile's equivalent of the US National Crime Victimization Survey — this week. Actual victimization rates edged slightly lower, but the perception of insecurity among Chileans remains stubbornly high. Analysts writing in El Mostrador note this gap — people feeling more scared even as crime statistics inch downward — is a defining feature of Kast's political environment. His government campaigned heavily on security, and the public keeps grading him on it even if the numbers move in the right direction.

Culture · Norte Grande

Chile's Great Desert Pilgrimage Just Wrapped — and This Year Marked 100 Years of the Virgin's Crown

The Fiesta de La Tirana, Chile's largest religious festival, concluded last week after ten days in the Atacama Desert. Between 250,000 and 500,000 pilgrims descended on a tiny town of a few hundred permanent residents in the Tarapacá region — about a two-hour drive from Iquique — to honor the Virgen del Carmen. The spectacle involves over 200 bailes religiosos (religious dance troupes) performing diabladas, morenadas, and tinkus in elaborate handmade costumes, often with enormous painted masks.

This year's edition had a special milestone: the centenary of the Virgin's coronation in 1926, prompting dance groups to commission a new crown made from materials symbolizing the pampa mining heritage. It's one of those events with no US equivalent — imagine Mardi Gras crossed with Burning Man and a deeply sincere Catholic pilgrimage, all in the middle of one of the world's driest deserts. The festival blends pre-Columbian Andean traditions, 19th-century silver-mine culture, and modern folk costumes into something Chile's anthropologists call a "living heritage."

"La agonía silenciosa de los hospitales públicos"

El Mostrador · Osvaldo Artaza · Left-leaning

Artaza, dean of a health faculty, argues Chile's public hospitals have entered a quiet crisis that the political noise is drowning out: stagnant productivity, waiting lists stretching years, and cost-per-procedure rising relative to the private sector. His argument is roughly: the government keeps talking about security spending while the health system is deteriorating without anyone declaring an emergency. The subtext is that Kast's budget cuts — however presented — land on a system already running on fumes. A reasonable left-center critique with solid data behind it.

Alex Anwandter — Twenty Years, One Sold-Out Arena

Chile's most influential indie-pop artist marks 20 years of work with a sold-out show at Movistar Arena on August 29 — a milestone that would be significant anywhere but is especially notable in a scene that historically exports its icons before celebrating them at home. Anwandter started as a teenage bedroom producer, built the cult project Teleradio Donoso, then went solo to become a festival fixture from Lollapalooza Chile to SXSW. His five solo albums blend queer visibility, political commentary, and meticulous production in a way that draws comparisons to Pet Shop Boys and Daft Punk at their most cerebral. The initial Movistar allocation sold in hours; a second release was also exhausted. If you have a ticket, the venue is Movistar Arena (Av. Beauchef 1100). If you don't, his catalog on streaming is a fine consolation.

Red Rockódromo 2026 — Chile's National Music Circuit, Now Underway

The Red Rockódromo launched July 10 in Atacama and tours all 16 Chilean regions through October, culminating at the 22nd Rockódromo Festival in Valparaíso. This year, 95 projects were selected from 1,628 applicants — a sort of rolling SXSW for acts that rarely get national exposure outside Santiago. The circuit covers rock, indie, folk, metal, and electronic — with stages in cities like Antofagasta, Concepción, Puerto Montt, and Punta Arenas that almost never see touring acts. It's the main pipeline for discovering who's coming up in Chilean music right now, and Rockaxis's coverage of the circuit is worth bookmarking.
