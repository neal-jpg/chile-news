What Is Codelco — and Why Does Its Future Matter?

Codelco (Corporación Nacional del Cobre de Chile) is the world's largest copper producer and is 100% owned by the Chilean government. It operates the most extensive known copper reserves on Earth — primarily in the Atacama Desert and the Andes — and has been state property since Salvador Allende nationalized the copper industry in 1971, a policy that survived even the Pinochet dictatorship.

How it works in Chile: Codelco generates billions of dollars in annual revenue that flows directly into the national budget. Historically, a fixed percentage of its profits was automatically earmarked for the Chilean military under the Ley Reservada del Cobre — a Pinochet-era law that wasn't repealed until 2019. Beyond its fiscal role, Codelco is a symbol of Chilean economic sovereignty: the nationalization of copper is widely taught in schools as a defining moment of modern Chilean identity, equivalent to how Americans view the creation of Social Security or the interstate highway system.

The American analogy: Imagine if the U.S. government fully owned and operated ExxonMobil, and a portion of its annual profits were constitutionally earmarked for the Pentagon. Then picture a conservative president's allies starting to suggest selling shares to private investors. That's roughly the magnitude of what's being discussed when Chilean commentators talk about privatizing Codelco.

Why it matters now (August 2026): President José Antonio Kast's government has pursued market-oriented reforms at pace — corporate tax cuts, deregulation, a push for private-sector investment. Some voices inside the governing coalition have begun floating the idea of opening Codelco to private capital, framing it as modernization. A column in The Clinic on August 1 warned that even tolerating the debate normalizes what was once unthinkable across the political spectrum. The left views any Codelco privatization as an attack on Chilean sovereignty; many center-right voters agree. But the Kast government's ideological direction has put the question — however quietly — back on the table.
