Politics

Kast's Security Reform Stalls in Senate After Civil-Liberties Backlash

President José Antonio Kast's proposed constitutional amendment — designed to let him deploy the military against organized crime for up to eight months without congressional approval — has hit a wall in Chile's Senate. The reform would create a brand-new "public-security state of exception," a constitutionally enshrined version of something like the U.S. Insurrection Act, allowing the executive to restrict movement, assembly, and other rights far longer than existing emergency frameworks permit. Opposition parties rejected it outright, and — more damaging for Kast — senators from his own center-right Chile Vamos coalition complained the powers were too sweeping, lacked oversight mechanisms, and echoed the tools of Chile's military dictatorship era.

By Wednesday, the government quietly removed the bill's urgency designation and signaled openness to modifications negotiated with Chile Vamos allies: congressional sign-off required from day one, 30-day review windows for renewals, and elimination of the most expansive provisions (communications interception, stripping social benefits from detainees). The reform now goes to Senate committees on Constitution, Security, and Defense simultaneously — a process that could take months. Kast, who ran on a tough-on-crime platform, needs a four-sevenths Senate supermajority (29 of 50 votes), a bar that looks hard to clear in the current form.

Economy

Budget 2027: Near-Zero Spending Growth as Unemployment Hits 5-Year High

Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz previewed the 2027 budget heading into formal congressional debate on September 30: spending growth of 0–1%, a real-terms freeze that signals Kast's commitment to fiscal austerity even in tough times. The backdrop is bleak — Chile's economy entered a technical recession in H1 2026 (two consecutive quarters of contraction), and unemployment has climbed to 9.4%, the highest in five years, with roughly a million people out of work. Think of it as the Kast equivalent of a Reagan-style "starve the beast" gambit: cut spending, trust that pro-market reforms eventually generate growth. Critics on the left say workers are the adjustment variable.

Economy

Megarreforma Signed into Law — But the Hard Work Is Just Starting

Chile's sweeping pro-market economic reform — the Megarreforma — cleared its final congressional hurdle this month when the Senate approved Kast's veto amendments, dispatching the bill to law. The reform includes corporate tax cuts and regulatory simplification intended to restart Chile's investment engine after years of stagnation under the previous center-left Boric government. Think of it as a Chilean version of Reagan's 1981 tax cuts: the theory is that lower friction and cheaper capital bring investment, which eventually creates jobs. Whether that theory plays out quickly enough to matter for unemployed Chileans is the central question of Kast's first year.

Interesting & Offbeat

Chile's Celestial August: Comet, Solar Eclipse, and Lunar Eclipse — All in One Month

August 2026 turned into a once-in-decades celestial triple-header for Southern Hemisphere observers. Comet P10/Tempel 2 reached perihelion (closest pass to the sun) on August 2 and its nearest approach to Earth on August 3, briefly visible to the naked eye from Chile's clear-sky latitudes. Days later, a total solar eclipse tracked across South America — Chile caught a partial view — followed by a lunar eclipse closing out the month. Chilean astronomers and amateur stargazers called it one of the richest single-month sky calendars in recent memory. If you've been too distracted by the news to look up lately, you've missed a show.

"Fuera la careta: el proyecto constitucional de Kast"

Read the fine print of Kast's security reform, this columnist argues, and you see not a crime-fighting tool but a framework for governing by exception — 240 days of unchecked military deployment, restricted movement, loss of social benefits for the accused. The piece contends that the president has used organized crime as the hook to pass powers that the Chilean right has wanted since returning to office: a constitutionalized version of extraordinary authority. "Chile has seen this playbook before," the column concludes — a reference no Chilean reader misses.

Libertad y Desarrollo · Right-leaning think tank · Aug 2026

"Seguridad Pública: La Otra Reforma"

Chile's leading free-market think tank makes the conservative case that the country's legal architecture is simply too slow to confront cartels and organized crime operating at institutional speed. The piece argues that critics are confusing "strong executive security powers with safeguards" and "authoritarianism without them" — and that the window to establish effective tools is narrow. Libertad y Desarrollo, roughly the Chilean equivalent of the Heritage Foundation, supports the reform's core intent while acknowledging that some of the drafting was too hasty and broad.
