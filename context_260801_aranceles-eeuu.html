U.S. Tariffs on Chile: What's Going On

The U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) — think of it as the trade arm of the U.S. executive branch — maintains a list of countries whose trade practices it deems problematic. In 2026, the Trump administration expanded a program targeting countries that allegedly allow goods made with forced labor to enter U.S. supply chains. Chile landed on that list, triggering an additional tariff of 12.5% on roughly 40% of its exports to the United States.

The logic: the USTR determined Chile lacks robust enough legal mechanisms to certify that its exported goods — particularly agricultural products and processed foods — are free of forced labor at some point in their supply chain. Chile disputes this designation; government officials point to existing labor law and prior trade commitments.

About 60% of Chilean exports to the U.S. are already exempt, mainly because they're raw commodities: refined copper, lithium carbonate, iodine, silver. The tariff hits harder on value-added goods: salmon, avocados, kiwis, citrus, wine, and wood products — products where Chile competes directly with other exporters.

American analogy: Imagine if Trump had slapped Canada with a 12% tariff in 2025 — not despite the USMCA free-trade agreement, but citing Canadian labor practices — even after Ottawa had spent years positioning itself as Washington's most loyal ally. That's roughly the position Kast's government finds itself in. The Kast administration explicitly modeled its foreign policy on close alignment with Trump, hoping ideological kinship would deliver preferential treatment. It didn't.

Why it matters now: Jeffrey Goettman, the #2 official at the USTR, is flying to Santiago in August 2026 for in-person negotiations — the first USTR high-level visit to South America in this administration. Chile is pushing to expand the exempt product list to include salmon, wine, and major fruit categories. Business associations and the Foreign Ministry are coordinating a joint negotiating strategy. The outcome will test whether pragmatic deal-making can achieve what political affinity could not.
