The 18-O Pardons Debate: Forgiveness or Impunity?

What is the 18-O?

On October 18, 2019 — "18-O" — a Santiago Metro fare hike sparked what became Chile's largest social uprising in decades. Millions took to the streets over months demanding change to a system many saw as deeply unequal: expensive private pensions, high healthcare costs, low wages. The protests caused widespread property destruction, but also led to significant violence by police and military against demonstrators. The official toll: 34 dead, 3,700 injured (many by rubber bullets or teargas), and hundreds of cases of serious abuse documented by the UN and Human Rights Watch.

One of the most prominent victims was Fabiola Campillai, a factory worker blinded in both eyes by a teargas canister fired point-blank by a Carabinero (Chile's national police). She later ran for Senate and won, becoming one of the most visible symbols of the social movement's grievances. The officer who blinded her was convicted and sentenced to prison.

What are the pardons about?

Courts have convicted dozens of police officers, soldiers, and members of the armed forces for crimes committed during the 18-O crackdown — including torture, unlawful killings, and serious bodily injury. President José Antonio Kast, who took office in March 2026, made pardoning these officers a campaign promise. His far-right coalition argues the officers were defending order against a chaotic uprising, that courts applied excessive charges, and that punishing security forces demoralizes the police and military.

Now, with the major economic reform law passed, Kast's allies are intensifying the pressure: 60 deputies signed a letter asking for individual presidential pardons, and Senate allies formed a formal "Caucus for General Pardon" pushing for a congressional law covering all convicted uniformed personnel.

The American analogy
Think of it like debating pardons for police officers convicted of brutality during the 2020 George Floyd protests — but amplified. The 18-O crackdown caused far more documented institutional violence than any single US protest response. Critics say pardoning the convicted officers would erase court-established accountability for human rights abuses. Supporters argue the security forces were doing their jobs under impossible conditions.
Why does it matter today?

The debate burst back into the open on August 5 in the Senate chamber itself, when Senator Campillai confronted Senator Camila Flores (RN, one of the pardon advocates) in an exchange that escalated to personal insults and is now under review by the Senate's Ethics Commission. Kast has not committed publicly — he wants to avoid a coalition split while managing the fragile rollout of his economic reform. But the pressure from his own base is intensifying by the week.
