Top Headlines
Security · Politics

Kast Backs Down on Security Reform After Cross-Party Revolt

President José Antonio Kast's government quietly retreated this week from its fastest-track push to pass a sweeping constitutional security overhaul, downgrading the ACOT reform from "suma urgencia" (a 15-day congressional deadline, roughly equivalent to invoking cloture in the US Senate) to a gentler 30-day "urgencia simple" effective September. Interior Minister Claudio Alvarado framed the move as openness to dialogue, saying the government wants an "agreed text" — but critics from both left and center-right read it as an acknowledgment that Kast simply does not have the votes.

The ACOT reform would embed public security in the constitution, create a new presidential state of exception targeting organized crime, and expand the military's domestic role — all without prior congressional approval for the emergency declaration. Constitutional experts from across the spectrum questioned whether those powers were even necessary. The retreat follows similar stumbles at the Constitutional Court and signals the central problem of Kast's first months: an executive that cannot reliably assemble majorities even among its own coalition.

Courts · Government

Constitutional Court Hands Kast a 9-1 Defeat on Disaster Utility Rules

On August 25, Chile's Constitutional Court (TC) rejected by a stunning 9-to-1 margin the Kast government's challenge to a provision requiring electricity, gas, and water companies to reconnect customers for free within 24–48 hours after a disaster declaration — even customers who were cut off for non-payment. The government had argued the article, embedded in the broad economic "mega-reform" law passed by the previous government, went beyond that law's stated scope.

The court didn't buy it. Coming on the same week as the ACOT retreat, the ruling deepened the narrative of an executive struggling at every institutional checkpoint. Small-business associations celebrated; power companies quietly absorbed the loss. Think of it as Chile's Supreme Court unanimously slapping down a presidential petition — the kind of lopsided defeat that sets a political tone.

Economy · Mining

Codelco Fined $1.5 Billion Pesos for Environmental Violations at Atacama Smelter

Chile's Environmental Court this week upheld a 1,850 UTA fine — roughly $1.5 billion Chilean pesos (about $1.6 million USD) — against state copper giant Codelco for violations at its Potrerillos smelter in the División El Salvador in Atacama. Inspectors found that Codelco had left copper concentrate slurry (think wet mining waste) outside its authorized closed-circuit containment and failed to clean two nearby ravines as required.

Codelco is Chile's state-owned copper miner — the biggest in the world — so fines against it carry both financial and symbolic weight. The case had been pending since the Environmental Superintendency first sanctioned the company, and the court's rejection of Codelco's appeal means the company must now pay up. Chile's copper industry is under increasing environmental scrutiny as climate pressure and global ESG standards tighten.

Economy · Congress

Kast's Economic Overhaul Cleared Congress — Now Awaits Constitutional Review

Chile's Congress in early August approved nearly all of President Kast's sweeping economic reform package — the biggest tax and regulatory overhaul since the 1990s. The centerpiece: gradually cutting the corporate tax rate from 27% to 23% by 2029, exempting newly built homes from VAT (Chile's sales tax), creating wage subsidies for employers who hire workers in a certain income band, and restricting which universities can participate in the free-tuition program.

Kast, a far-right leader who took office in March promising to reverse what he called socialist overreach, is betting the cuts will jolt an economy that shrank 0.5% in Q1 and where unemployment hit 9.4% — the highest since 2021. But the law cannot be officially promulgated yet: opposition lawmakers filed constitutional challenges, so it still sits with the TC while the ACOT drama plays out in parallel.

Film · Heritage

Film Students Rescue a Forgotten 1961 Chilean Classic

Seven film students at the Universidad de Chile — as part of a regular semester course — completed the frame-by-frame digital restoration of Alias Gardelito, a 1961 black-and-white feature widely considered a touchstone of Chilean cinema but barely screened for decades due to deterioration. The restored version is slated for a public showing this September in Santiago, making it accessible to audiences for the first time in a generation.

The film is named after a small-time drifter who idolizes the legendary Argentine tango singer Carlos Gardel — think a Chilean On the Waterfront filtered through 1960s Latin noir. The restoration was funded through a Ciencia Pública grant, a nice reminder that some university coursework produces things that actually matter.

Opinion & Editorial
El Líbero · Right-leaning · Cristóbal Yuraszeck Krebs

"Chile counts the cost to the state — but who counts the cost to the rest of us?"

Yuraszeck argues that Chile has a long-established practice of calculating the "fiscal cost" of new legislation — what it will cost the government — but almost never estimates the private compliance burden: the paperwork, legal fees, administrative overhead, and operational constraints that new rules impose on businesses and citizens. He uses the ACOT reform and the mega-reform debate as a jumping-off point to argue for a mandatory regulatory impact assessment on all new laws, a reform that the Kast government has gestured toward but not yet implemented.

"Two losses in one week: Kast's bad August keeps getting worse"

The Clinic's editorial desk surveys the ACOT retreat and the 9-1 TC ruling and draws a pointed conclusion: Kast arrived in March with a mandate and a hardline agenda, but five months in he is losing at the courts, losing in Congress, and losing the narrative. The editorial argues that a right-wing executive that can't pass its own flagship security bill through a right-friendly Senate is not a governing problem — it's a legitimacy problem, and one that won't be fixed by moving to a slower urgency track.
