What Is a "Technical Recession" — and Is Chile in One?

A technical recession is defined as two consecutive quarters of negative economic growth — meaning the economy actually shrank, quarter over quarter (or year over year, depending on the methodology), for six straight months. Chile's Banco Central confirmed on August 18, 2026 that GDP fell 0.2% in the second quarter (April–June), following a downwardly revised 0.3% contraction in the first quarter (January–March). That makes it official: Chile is in a technical recession for the first time since the first half of 2023.

How Chile tracks it. Chile's Banco Central publishes quarterly national accounts data — the equivalent of what the US Bureau of Economic Analysis reports every quarter as GDP. Two straight quarters of negative growth triggers the "technical recession" label. Unlike the US, where the official arbiter is the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) — which looks at a broader basket of indicators including employment, income, and industrial output — Chile uses the simpler two-quarter rule as its conventional marker.

American analogy. In mid-2022, the US briefly had two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth and a heated debate erupted about whether America was technically in a recession. The Biden administration and many economists argued it wasn't, because employment was strong. Chile faces a similar debate now, but with one additional complication: unemployment has climbed to its highest level since 2021, which makes the "it's not really a recession" argument harder to sustain.

What caused it. The driver is copper — Chile's economic engine, accounting for roughly a quarter of all exports. Mining activity fell 6.4% in Q2 2026 as global copper prices softened and several large deposits underperformed. Construction contracted 3.2%, investment stagnated, and while household consumption grew a modest 1.2%, it wasn't enough to offset the drag. Analysts surveyed by the Banco Central now project full-year 2026 growth of around 1%.

Why it matters today. President Kast took office in March 2026 on an explicit promise of economic reactivation. His signature corporate tax cut package — designed to attract private investment — cleared Congress in July, but the GDP data suggests it hasn't yet produced results. With approval at 35% and a majority of Chileans saying the country is going backwards, the recession confirms the political headwind Kast is navigating heading into the second half of his first year.
