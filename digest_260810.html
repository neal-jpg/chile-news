Economy · Legislation

Chile's Congress Approves Kast's Sweeping Tax Reform — Government Already Preparing Vetoes

Chile's Senate voted 27–22 last week to clear the final piece of President José Antonio Kast's flagship "mega-reform," sending to the executive a law that cuts the corporate income tax rate from 27% to 23% — bringing it in line with the OECD average and fulfilling a central campaign promise. The package also includes a full tax integration system (letting shareholders credit corporate taxes against personal taxes) and investor-stability contracts locking in rates for 10, 15, or 20 years depending on investment size.

The win came with asterisks. During its long legislative journey, the opposition amended the bill to add three consumer-protection measures — a debt-forgiveness window for small borrowers, a ban on compound interest (charging interest on unpaid interest), and a 30-day payment guarantee for small businesses. The government immediately announced presidential vetoes on all three, calling them distortions that could create moral hazard or harm credit markets. The Autonomous Fiscal Council — Chile's equivalent of the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office — said the bill produces a "net negative fiscal impact," and the IMF warned the plan is ambitious given Chile's structural deficit. Kast's team argues the growth it will unlock — projecting a rise from 2.5% GDP growth to 4% by end of term — will more than offset the revenue dip.

Security · Politics

Kast Unveils 30-Measure Anti-Crime Plan, Including a New Constitutional Amendment

In a presentation at La Moneda last Thursday, Kast announced a 30-plus initiative security package and — in a politically striking move — proposed amending Chile's constitution to explicitly enshrine public safety as a state duty. Key measures include extending the "flagrancy window" for arrests from 12 to 24 hours (think of it as widening the window for warrantless detention when police catch someone in the act), stretching migrant detention during deportation proceedings from 5 to 60 days, and creating a new category of emergency security decree for areas overrun by criminal gangs.

The left called it punitive theater. Communist Party legislator Bernardo Salinas said the government is pursuing "an ideological, punitive agenda" while refusing to touch banking secrecy laws that shield white-collar crime. Critics on that side also noted the irony: Chile already had two failed constitutional conventions in 2022 and 2023 — the right spent years opposing constitutional rewrites — and now it is Kast himself launching one, solely on the security chapter.

Politics · Justice

60 Ruling-Party Lawmakers Sign Letter Demanding Kast Pardon Officers Convicted in 2019 Uprising

Almost five months into his term, Kast has yet to exercise the presidential pardon power he promised to use for Carabineros (national police) and military officers convicted of human-rights violations during Chile's explosive 2019 social uprising — the estallido. Sixty deputies from the government coalition signed a letter this week demanding action, as right-leaning caucuses are growing impatient. The 2019 protests were a roughly six-week national explosion — picture a nationwide George Floyd moment but triggered by a subway fare hike — that left dozens dead and hundreds accused of rights abuses on both sides.

The opposition is countering with its own constitutional reform, which would bar the president from issuing individual pardons for crimes against humanity. That reform is still in committee. Kast faces a classic squeeze: the base wants the pardons now; the courts and international human-rights bodies would treat them as a red line.

Economy · Housing

Senate Clears Property-Tax Relief for Seniors — A Small Piece of a Contentious Tax Bill

Buried inside the tax reform vote was an article that reduces property-tax ("contribuciones") bills for Chileans over 65 on their primary home. The Senate approved a revenue-neutral mechanism to compensate municipalities for the lost income, ending a weeks-long standoff over how to fund the rebate. For context, Chilean property taxes are paid quarterly and are a significant household expense — a perennial source of political tension, especially for fixed-income retirees in gentrifying neighborhoods like Ñuñoa or Providencia.

El Mostrador · Left-leaning · Aug 7

"Kast's Great Constitutional Innovation on Security Is Already in the Constitution"

An El Mostrador analysis argues that Kast's proposed constitutional amendment — enshrining public safety as a state duty — is legally redundant: the existing 1980 constitution already contains virtually identical language, approved through decades of court interpretation and prior amendments. The piece frames the initiative as "pirotecnia constitucional" (constitutional fireworks): politically satisfying, substantively empty, and ironic coming from a president whose coalition spent four years blocking any new constitutional text. The author contends the real policy work is in the 30 legislative measures — the constitutional piece is theater.

El Líbero · Right-leaning · Aug 9

"The Left's Bad Faith on Security Reform"

Conservative historian and columnist Alejandro San Francisco, writing in El Líbero, argues that the left's coordinated attacks on Kast's security package reveal ideological reflex rather than substantive critique. He notes Chile's homicide rate has doubled in a decade — driven in part by the Tren de Aragua and other transnational gangs — and contends that opposing longer flagrancy windows or faster deportations while crime rises amounts to protecting a process over the safety of Chileans. The column is a classic law-and-order argument: the equivalent of a Wall Street Journal editorial saying soft-on-crime policies have consequences.

Kids 🌲 Outdoors

San Cristóbal Hill — Teleférico & Parquemet (Ongoing)

Parque Metropolitano (free entry, Mon–Sun 6–19h) connects the city via two cable-car lines: the classic Pío Nono–to-summit route and the newer Teleférico II, which links the Pedro de Valdivia Norte access point to Plaza México near the hilltop. Both cabins join near the summit. On a clear winter afternoon, the panorama of Santiago against the snow-dusted Andes is hard to beat.

The park zoo, pools (closed in winter), and botanical garden are on site. Best for families on a clear mid-week morning — weekends can be busy. Teleférico has its own ticket; check telefericosantiago.cl for current prices and hours before heading out.

Rock Indie

Hesse Kassel — Morir Saltando

Released in July, the six-piece's second album is their biggest swing yet. Where their debut "La Brea" built a reputation on melodic, mid-tempo rock, "Morir Saltando" goes rawer and more physical — the band recorded it live, all six members playing simultaneously in the same room to capture the energy they bring on stage. The result is a record that sounds like it's about to tip over in a good way. Their Lollapalooza Chile set earlier this year turned heads; this album is the follow-through. For fans of: Los Blops-era grit meets early Los Prisioneros urgency.

Rock Indie

Candelabro — New Lineup, Pulsar Sweep, and International Dates

Chile's most talked-about rock band had a complicated summer. In June, founding guitarist Luis Ayala and multi-instrumentalist María Lobos departed; they were replaced by David Romero on guitar and — in the most surprising move — Isidora Aldonza on oboe. An oboist in a rock band: the move is either eccentric or visionary, and from this group it's probably both. The disruption didn't slow their momentum: Candelabro won the Pulsar Award (Chile's Grammy equivalent) for Best Rock Album and took home the top prize, Album of the Year, for their 2025 LP "Deseo, carne y voluntad." International dates in Peru and Spain are confirmed, and they headline Festival Neutral on September 26.
