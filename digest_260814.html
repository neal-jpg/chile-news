Top Headlines
Security

Chile Sends 295 High-Risk Inmates to "South America's Most Modern Prison" in Kast's Biggest Security Show Yet

President José Antonio Kast personally traveled to La Laguna prison in Talca — about 250 kilometers south of Santiago — on Wednesday to oversee the transfer of 295 high-risk inmates from jails across the country, including 37 members of organized crime organizations. La Laguna, billed by the government as South America's most modern facility, holds 2,320 prisoners in individual cells, with communication-jamming technology, eight guard towers, reinforced surveillance, and systems designed to prevent incarcerated gang leaders from running their operations remotely.

The high-profile transfer is a flagship move of Kast's Agenda against Organized Crime and Terrorism (ACOT) — his signature security push unveiled in a national broadcast last week. It comes under real political pressure: Kast fired his first security minister just two months into his term, and his government has deported fewer than 900 of the estimated 300,000 undocumented immigrants without legal status — far short of campaign pledges. Supporters see La Laguna as proof of momentum; the left calls it political theater timed to dominate the news cycle before ACOT's congressional debate begins.

Politics

Kast Signs Security Constitutional Reform; Congress Given Christmas Deadline for 31-Bill ACOT Package

President Kast signed a constitutional reform Monday that would embed public security as a core obligation in Article 1 of Chile's constitution — the equivalent of writing "homeland security" into the preamble of the US Constitution. The reform anchors ACOT's broader 31-bill package, which includes a national registry of criminal organizations, a new focused state of exception (think a targeted version of martial law), and expanded prison oversight powers.

The heads of both chambers called the package an urgent legislative priority. The government wants everything passed by Christmas, betting that high public support for security measures will fracture the opposition — some center-left legislators are willing to back the reforms; the Communist Party and progressive left are raising civil liberties concerns about expanded state powers.

Economy

Tax Mega-Reform Clears Congress — But Three Presidential Vetoes and a Constitutional Court Review Still Stand Between Law and Reality

The Senate approved Kast's centerpiece economic reform on August 4 (27–22), reducing the corporate tax rate from 27% to 23% over several years and rewriting 36 laws and 15 decrees to attract investment. Finance Minister Quiroz declared that "Chile opens its doors to investment again." The center-left opposition called it a "black day," arguing the law favors large businesses and shortchanges municipalities that lost property-tax revenue in the process.

But the law isn't settled yet. The government plans to veto three clauses the opposition added during debate, and Chile's Constitutional Tribunal — imagine the US Supreme Court combined with a legislative referee — is reviewing environmental and tax provisions separately. Most measures won't fully take effect until 2027–2029.

Housing

Congress Unanimously Expands Homebuying Subsidy: 30,000 New Slots, Higher Eligible Price Cap, Deadline Extended to 2028

A unanimous congressional vote last week expanded Chile's main homebuying subsidy program — adding 30,000 new spots, raising the cap on eligible homes to 6,000 UF (roughly $200,000 USD at current exchange rates), and extending the application window to May 2028. Chile's housing market runs heavily on government subsidy programs, which function a bit like FHA loans in the US but are structurally more central: most middle-income families depend on subsidies to access ownership at all. The expansion gives more households a path forward as urban prices continue climbing.

Archaeology

Chilean Mummy Yields the First Genetic Proof That Smallpox Arrived in the Americas with the Conquistadors

Scientists studying 13 bone samples from a naturally mummified body found on Chile's northern Pacific coast extracted DNA of the smallpox virus — providing the first hard genetic evidence that European conquerors brought the disease that devastated Indigenous populations across the Americas. Written chronicles and population records had long pointed to catastrophic epidemics after 1492, but physical proof of the virus itself had been missing. This mummy closes that evidentiary gap. The finding was published this week and is drawing wide attention from international archaeology and genetics communities.

Left · Civil Liberties

"Does Defending Rights Mean Being on the Side of Criminals?"

El Clarín (left-leaning) · August 11, 2026

As ACOT advances in Congress, Republican deputy Agustín Romero accused the Progressive Front and Communist Party of being "on the side of criminals" for questioning expanded police powers in the reform package. The column argues this framing — Americans will recognize it from the 1990s "tough on crime" era — is being weaponized to shut down legitimate debate about due process, civil liberties, and whether handing police broader authority actually reduces organized crime. The author warns Chile risks repeating patterns where security panic crowds out constitutional guardrails, with the real losers being ordinary citizens rather than the gangsters the law is aimed at.

Right · Democratic Standards

"The Challenge of Countering Extremism From Both Ends"

El Líbero (right-leaning) · Víctor Maldonado · August 13, 2026

Maldonado argues that Chile's real democratic challenge isn't simply stopping left-wing extremism — it's demonstrating that measured conservative governance actually delivers results. Writing against the backdrop of ACOT's congressional debut, he worries that hardliners inside the ruling coalition are undermining the credibility of liberal-democratic conservatism by framing every policy disagreement as moral treachery. The piece calls for political standards that attract moderates, warning that extremism on both ends feeds each other — and that the center holds only if it can prove it works.

Rock

Angelo Pierattini — "No Estoy Muerto, Espérame"

Released July 23, this comeback single from one of Chile's most beloved singer-songwriters carries its full weight in the title: "I'm Not Dead, Wait for Me." Pierattini suffered a serious accident that left him in a coma before recording this track, and it shows — intimate, direct, and emotionally raw in a way that few Chilean rock records manage. A significant moment in a long career. For fans of León Larregui, Fito Páez, or any confessional singer-songwriter who performs as though the song costs them something.
